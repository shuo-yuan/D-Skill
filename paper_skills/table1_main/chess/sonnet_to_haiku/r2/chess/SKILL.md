## Critical Rules (MUST follow)


- If any tool output (`<board>`, `<safety>`, `<simulate>`) flags your own piece as "en prise" or a destination as "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK", do NOT commit that move yet. First check the attacker (via `<hanging>` or `<safety>`) and run `<simulate>`/`<line>` through the actual capture to see resulting material and status. Only commit if the forced follow-up still leaves you equal/ahead, or a concrete checked alternative is worse.
- Never commit a move on verbal justification alone ("this is forced/unstoppable") when budget remains — trace the forced opponent reply (and your own follow-up) with `<simulate>`/`<line>` to a concrete end-state and read the printed material/en-prise/status fields before committing. If your `<simulate>`/`<line>` calls on that move keep erroring, fix the syntax and retry rather than falling back to mental analysis — commit only once you have at least one successful (non-error) result showing the resulting board/material for the line you intend to play.

- "No hanging piece / no losing exchange" from `<safety>` alone is NOT sufficient to commit — that only confirms absence of immediate loss. Require a `<line>`/`<simulate>` result showing a concrete positive outcome (material gain or checkmate) for your intended move plus forced reply plus follow-up before committing.

- A `#` suffix in the legal-move list is a hypothesis, not proof of checkmate — this holds even if a reference/expert trace you are imitating played it with no verification; copying an unverified commit is still a gap to avoid, not a pattern to repeat. NEVER call `<action>` directly off a `#`/`+` annotation. First state in one line, for EVERY adjacent square the enemy king could move to, the exact piece/pawn that blocks or covers it (e.g. "e8 blocked by my bishop, g8 covered by rook h8, e7/f7/g7 blocked by own pawns") — a vague label like "king is trapped" without naming the specific blocker per square is NOT sufficient and is a signal to verify with a tool instead of trusting the label. To confirm: call `<simulate>`/`<board>` on the move ALONE with NO guessed opponent replies appended (e.g. `<simulate>Nh6</simulate>`, not `<simulate>Nh6, Kg7, Kh8</simulate>`) — you do not know the legal replies in advance, so never invent them; read whether the tool reports zero legal replies / CHECKMATE / game-over for that resulting position. If that call errors (e.g., "not a legal move" or similar), fix the exact syntax issue and immediately re-issue a corrected call with remaining budget — do NOT fall back to committing on the `#` label alone just because a call errored. Only skip the confirming call entirely if budget is fully exhausted.
- Before committing ANY forcing move (check or material-winning capture), run `<simulate>`/`<line>` on it first and read the opponent's forced reply and resulting material/en-prise state — never commit from a mental read alone. This applies even when the move looks obviously winning.

- After a pawn/piece trade shows a one-sided material swing (e.g., "you are down 1"), do NOT treat that as the verdict — check whether any of your pieces (especially the king in an endgame) can recapture on the very next move, and extend the line one more ply (`<line>`) through that recapture before judging the trade good or bad.
- If a verification call on your intended move errors out, returns an illegal-move result, or shows a result that contradicts your hypothesis (opponent has non-mating replies, your piece ends up en prise, follow-up move is illegal), treat this as DISPROOF of the line, not an obstacle to route around — do NOT commit the move anyway "because it looks forcing." Search for a different candidate instead.
- HARD VETO: if ANY branch of your own already-run `<simulate>`/`<safety>` output for the exact candidate shows a materially worse result (e.g., "down 5") on a reply the opponent could actually choose, that candidate is DISPROVEN regardless of a better-looking branch elsewhere in the same output — do not write a WHY that only discusses the favorable branch. Before `<action>`, re-scan ALL reply branches in the most recent tool output for your candidate and confirm the WORST one is still acceptable.

- Never call a tool on a candidate move string until you first confirm it appears verbatim in the current "Legal moves" list — if it isn't there, discard the idea instead of spending a call to discover it's illegal.
- Even when a move looks forced/obvious (resolving check, recapturing a hanging piece), spend one `<safety>`/`<board>` call on it before `<action>` and confirm "forcing replies: none" and "your pieces en prise: none" — skip only if tool budget for that move is fully exhausted.
- Every turn, before shortlisting any candidate, call `<threats>` (or `<hanging>`) to see what the opponent threatens if you pass. If a mate threat against your own king is returned, restrict your candidates to moves that block/capture/give-check against it, and re-run `<threats>`/`<safety>` on the shortlisted candidate to confirm the mate threat is actually gone — a move that merely looks forcing is not a verified defense.
- Never commit the first move of a multi-move forcing sequence after verifying only one ply. After `<simulate>` returns the opponent's forced reply, continue with `<line>` through your intended follow-up until the output reaches a terminal state (CHECKMATE, STALEMATE, or no forcing moves remain) — "material is okay after one reply" is not sufficient to commit.
- Even when your mental read of the board feels certain (e.g., "king boxed in, this is mate"), still make the confirming tool call before `<action>` — a correct-looking read is not a substitute for a cited tool verdict; commit only after reading "CHECKMATE"/"no legal replies" (or equivalent) in actual tool output.

- If a `<safety>`/`<line>` mate claim's output is incomplete or ambiguous (e.g., missing an explicit "forcing replies: none"/"CHECKMATE" string, or you can't confirm attacker/defender counts at the destination favor you), do NOT commit on that single read — issue one more confirming call (e.g., `<line>` replaying the position after the move) before `<action>`.

- Once a tool call (`<safety>`/`<simulate>`/`<line>`) explicitly returns a terminal verdict ("CHECKMATE", "STALEMATE", or "no legal replies") for your exact candidate move, commit that move immediately — cite the tool's literal verdict as your WHY justification and do NOT spend an extra step re-deriving or restating the conclusion from your own board read. Play the move string exactly as it appears in the legal-move list, including any `#`/`+` suffix the tool/list confirmed.
- If your chosen verification tool errors not from a fixable syntax mistake but from a structural limitation (e.g., `<line>` demands at least 2 moves, a tool unavailable while in check), do NOT fall back to committing on board-reading intuition — immediately try a DIFFERENT applicable tool (e.g., switch from `<line>` to `<board>`/`<safety>`/`<simulate>`) to get an explicit confirmatory result. Before `<action>`, confirm at least one tool call this turn returned a successful non-error result explicitly supporting the move — if every attempt errored, try one more alternative tool before committing.


- Proven opening habit: call `<threats>` first on every new move (before any candidate generation) to see the opponent's forcing replies/captures if you passed — this consistently correctly shapes which candidates matter and which of your pieces are at risk.
## Finding the Move: Motifs and Candidates



Before picking any candidate, enumerate EVERY move in the legal-move list annotated with "+" or "#" plus every capture, and briefly note why each non-chosen check/mate was rejected (e.g., doesn't mate, loses material) — this makes the choice a systematic scan result, not a lucky glance at one marked move.

Runtime check: literally scan the raw legal-move list string for a "#" character BEFORE any piece-by-piece reasoning — a "#"-marked move is engine-confirmed mate and is your first candidate, found faster than deriving it manually. Still make the single confirming `<board>`/`<line>` call before `<action>` (per the Critical Rules checkmate check), but do not waste reasoning steps re-deriving what the annotation already tells you.


Before calling any tool, scan the legal-move list and board for check indicators (only king moves legal, or an opponent piece attacking your king along an open file/rank/diagonal) and state this in your WHY line. If you are in check, skip tools that require "not in check" (e.g., `<threats>` will error) and go straight to `<safety>`/`<simulate>` on king moves or blocking/capturing moves instead.

Proven pattern (confirmed across many successful runs): open EVERY move's analysis with `<threats>` to list forcing replies/threats, then immediately batch all forcing-looking candidates (checks, captures, back-rank ideas) into one `<safety>` call to compare check status and exchange risk side by side before deepening any single one with `<simulate>`/`<line>`.

Before generating candidates — including on your very first move of the game — call `<threats>` with no argument first, then `<hanging>` (leading with `<hanging>` instead is an equally proven alternative ordering), to see what the opponent would do if you passed (loose pieces, mate threats, forcing replies) and which of your own pieces are already en prise — this baseline, proven across many successful runs, tells you what your candidates must neutralize or outweigh. NEVER skip this opening `<threats>` call just because you already spotted an attacking idea of your own (e.g., a check) — a missed opponent threat (like a pawn one step from promoting and forking two of your pieces) can outweigh any attack you find, and your final move choice/WHY must explicitly address every threat `<threats>` returns, not just your own candidate's upside. Runtime check: after this bare `<threats>` call, explicitly compare your eventual candidate's effect against EACH listed opponent threat (not just the scariest one) before moving to deeper simulation — the candidate must neutralize them or produce a bigger threat of its own. Keep that forcing-reply list visible (restate it mentally) as you evaluate every later candidate, and when you read a `<safety>`/`<simulate>` result, explicitly confirm it addresses one of those listed opponent replies, not an unrelated or weaker one. Explicitly scan the output for a "mate threats" line — if the opponent has a mate threat against you, restrict your candidate list to moves that block, capture, or otherwise directly neutralize that exact threat before considering anything else. If the output confirms "mate threats: none," proceed directly to attacking/forcing candidates without spending calls on extra defensive checks. Then call `<hanging>` to check which of YOUR OWN pieces (and the opponent's) are attacked or undefended. Only after confirming your own pieces are safe (or you have a forcing resource that neutralizes the threat) proceed — anchor your first candidate to a specific piece/square named in that output.
 Treat every undefended enemy piece named in `<hanging>`'s output as an immediate capture candidate, not just background information — add it to your candidate list before brainstorming quiet or speculative moves. Proven sequence: call `<threats>` first to confirm passing wouldn't let the opponent play something worse, THEN run `<safety>` on the exact capture to confirm zero net attackers at the destination and no newly en-prise piece on your side — only then commit. Carry this habit into every later `<safety>`/`<board>`/`<simulate>` call by explicitly reading the "en prise"/exchange-risk field before judging a candidate safe.

When scanning for forcing candidates, check every piece type that can give check or restrict the king (queen, rook, minor pieces) — do not anchor your first `<safety>` batch to only one piece type. If every checking move from one piece comes back flagged "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK"/en-prise, immediately broaden to checks/forks from other pieces in the legal-move list rather than retrying variations on the same flagged piece.

Then name the concrete tactical motif suggested by piece placement (fork, pin, discovered check, back-rank threat, etc.), and scan the legal-move list for forcing moves tied to that motif before considering quiet moves. First ask "does any move end in # or +?" — a `#`-annotated mate, or any move directly attacking a king confined by its own pawns/pieces, is top priority to verify first, but do not lock onto it blindly: also enumerate every other check and capture as candidates, since a puzzle can contain a trap or multiple forcing options. Confirm a mating candidate by checking the tool output's "gives check"/"CHECKMATE" field before committing, not by WHY-note reasoning alone.

When `<hanging>` flags one of your own pieces as en prise, do not just pick the "most forcing" candidate (check/capture) and hope the opponent ignores the hanging piece. Enumerate ALL of that piece's legal destinations (not just the obvious capture/push) AND any other candidate that defends/blocks the exposure, run `<safety>` on them together, and prefer a move that removes the exposure structurally (e.g., the capturing reply becomes illegal or loses for the opponent in post-move `<simulate>`) over a forcing move whose `<simulate>` output still shows a branch where the opponent can safely take the hanging piece — a retreat/defending square with 0 attackers can beat a tempting capture.

Also check captures of enemy pieces: call `<hanging>` at least once per move decision (not optionally) to get the concrete list of pieces en prise on both sides — never substitute a subjective read like "king trapped" or "devastating threats" for this concrete check. Use the returned list to generate or filter candidates and verify via `<safety>`/`<board>` whether the target square has more attackers than defenders. Treat "capture a hanging enemy piece (especially with check)" as a first-class candidate category alongside checks — scan for it BEFORE iterating only on checks — and compare its material outcome against your best check line before choosing. Treat a verified free/winning capture as the default move; only override it with a check if `<simulate>`/`<line>` confirms that line wins more material or forces mate. Before selecting any forcing candidate (check, mate threat, or capture), state the structural reason it works — which escape squares the enemy king lacks, which of your pieces defend the mating/target square, and what boxes the king in — do not select a move merely because the legal-move list shows a `+`/`#` marker; verify the geometry yourself first. When your structural read identifies ONE check/capture that fits a named mating pattern (king boxed in by its own pieces, no escape squares), test that single candidate first with one `<line>` call through the full forced sequence BEFORE batch-testing other checks with `<safety>` — do not parallel-test 2-3 checks via `<safety>` just to compare surface-level exchange-risk flags, that burns calls without confirming any one line's actual terminal result.


Once `<threats>` identifies a concrete threat against you (especially a mate threat), immediately generate only candidates that directly neutralize it (block the line, capture/pin the attacker, interpose on the mating square) and batch-test those together in one `<safety>` call. Do not spend calls re-testing already-rejected moves, issuing unrelated exploratory batches, or probing malformed move strings — this wastes budget that should instead fund a final `<simulate>` confirming the chosen defense survives the opponent's best reply.

For every candidate you consider against a reported mate threat, do not assume a check or capture defuses it just because it looks forcing — run `<line>`/`<threats>` one ply past that exact candidate and confirm in the output that the original mating move is no longer legal/available. Only commit once a tool result explicitly shows the mate threat is gone; a candidate that doesn't address the mating square/attacker is irrelevant no matter how forcing it looks.



Before spending a `<simulate>`/`<line>` call, state the specific double-purpose motif the candidate exploits (e.g., "check that also attacks the undefended rook on d8" — a fork) AND name the exact defender that is pinned, overloaded, or missing and why the opponent's natural recapture/defense is unavailable (e.g., "knight cannot reach g6; pawn's only defender is gone"). Do NOT simulate a candidate with no stated tactical reason; guessing-and-checking burns budget and risks illegal-move errors. Runtime check: if you can name the immobilized/missing defender before touching a tool, test at most 1-2 candidates with `<safety>`/`<simulate>` — needing to batch 3+ candidates is a signal your WHY line is too vague and you are letting the tool do the generation work instead of your own read. After each `<simulate>` result, ask "does this already show forced material win or mate?" — if yes, stop generating new unrelated candidates and move straight to confirming/committing that line instead of testing more moves.

Screen every forcing candidate (checks/captures) with `<safety>` before deciding, and treat any "attackers > defenders" or "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK" flag as a hard filter to drop that candidate — batch all candidates in one call, discard every flagged one, and choose among the surviving zero-attacker/zero-en-prise candidates rather than settling for the first flagged option. Re-run `<safety>` on your final chosen move immediately before committing, even if it already passed screening earlier — the position may have shifted during deeper analysis.


When `<hanging>` reports an opponent piece en prise (a free capture available), treat capturing it as a top-priority candidate and run `<safety>`/`<simulate>` on that exact capture before committing to ANY other move — do not let a flagged free-material option silently drop from consideration. Only override it if that check shows the destination is actually defended or leads to a worse forced sequence.

Triage phase (do this first, every move): call `<threats>` then `<hanging>` to map loose/threatened pieces on both sides, then run `<safety>` on your top 2-3 candidates together to filter out any flagged `[LOSING EXCHANGE RISK]` or en-prise moves — only proceed to `<simulate>`/`<line>` on the surviving candidates.

Runtime check for king safety: before generating candidates, count the opponent king's escape squares from the printed board. If 2 or fewer are free (the rest occupied by the king's own pieces/pawns), treat the king as boxed/exposed — scan the FULL legal-move list for every check and capture near that king, list them all as candidates, and verify each with `<safety>`/`<simulate>`/`<line>` before choosing among them (this batch-then-verify approach has repeatedly identified the winning line in boxed-king positions).


When `<threats>`/`<hanging>` names a specific opponent forcing reply that wins material from you (e.g., "Bxf4 captures queen"), treat neutralizing that exact threat (retreat, defend, block, trade off the attacker) as a REQUIRED candidate property — discard any candidate, however forcing-looking, that leaves the named threat fully intact, before even running `<safety>` on it.

Proven pattern for choosing among multiple checking candidates (including the opening move of a game): batch ALL checking options into one `<safety>` call, discard any flagged "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK" or with pieces en prise, then confirm the single surviving candidate with one `<board>`/`<line>` call showing "your side en prise: none" before committing — do not skip the final confirming call even if the candidate is the only one left standing.


Pin/skewer check: when `<threats>`/`<board>` shows an opponent queen or piece sharing a diagonal, file, or rank with its own king, explicitly name that alignment (e.g., "queen on a7-g1 diagonal with own king") and treat the move that attacks along that exact line as a top candidate before scanning other ideas. For that candidate, batch-simulate ALL of the opponent's forced retreat/reply options (not just the first one or two) — a pin is only confirmed winning once every checked reply still loses material or stays pinned.

Fork checkpoint: when a knight (or any piece) move gives check, also check if it simultaneously attacks another undefended enemy piece (a fork). If so, run `<safety>`/`<simulate>` on the check and confirm every legal king reply still leaves "your pieces en prise: none" and the forked piece undefended — then play the check first and the capture as the follow-up move.


Before any `<safety>`/`<simulate>` batch call, split the legal-move list into forcing (checks/captures/direct threats) vs. quiet/speculative moves. In short-horizon puzzles (1-2 move mates), test ONLY the forcing moves first — do not include a speculative quiet move (e.g., a repositioning piece move with no check/capture) in the same batch just because it came to mind; this wastes a call on a move likely to be flagged en-prise with no useful information. Add quiet candidates to a later batch only if all forcing moves fail to produce a forced, verifiable continuation.

Sacrifice scan: before narrowing to check-only candidates, scan the full legal-move list for every CAPTURE landing on or adjacent to the enemy king (not just checks) — a capturing sac (e.g., Bxg7 next to a castled king) can open lines a simple check can't. Run at least one `<simulate>`/`<line>` on each such capture before picking a final candidate; skipping captures near the king to focus only on checks has caused missed forced-mate lines.


If a checking/forcing candidate's `<simulate>` result confirms a losing material swing, do NOT immediately try another flagged-risky check — first re-examine the `<hanging>` output from this turn for an undefended enemy piece (especially one capturable with check) and verify/compare that free capture's outcome before investing more calls in risky sacrifices.

Priority check when BOTH a hanging piece AND an exposed enemy king are present: before committing to "just capture the free piece," first enumerate every check available in the legal-move list (the king's exposure often enables a forcing sequence stronger than any single capture). Test those checks with `<safety>`/`<simulate>` alongside the capture candidate, and only settle on the plain capture once you've confirmed via tool output that no check leads to an equal-or-better (especially mating) result.

Hanging-piece-first rule: if `<threats>`/`<hanging>` reports a capture available against one of YOUR OWN pieces (not just enemy pieces), treat resolving that exact threat (move it, defend it, or trade it off) as a REQUIRED candidate to test before spending budget on speculative attacking sacrifices elsewhere on the board — do not chase a mating idea on the opposite wing while your own piece remains hanging and unaddressed.
## Verifying the Move: Tool Ritual



Before any tool call, cross-check every candidate move string against the printed "Legal moves" list (or latest board state); never guess a destination square, piece reach, or follow-up ply — an illegal-move guess burns a call for zero new information.
 For moves needing disambiguation (two rooks/knights that can reach the same square, e.g. Rcc1 vs Rdc1), copy the exact disambiguated string from the "Legal moves" list rather than inventing your own shorthand — an ambiguous or wrong-file/rank move string fails and wastes a call without new information. When a tool response lists the opponent's actual legal replies, use that list directly to pick your next candidates instead of guessing from memory.

If a tool call errors (syntax, "illegal move", or a guessed opponent reply like a wrong king square), diagnose the SPECIFIC cause before retrying
Before typing ANY opponent-reply guess into `<simulate>`/`<line>`, prefer calling `<board>` on your candidate alone first (or reuse a legal-reply list already printed by `<threats>`/`<safety>`/a prior error) to get the actual legal replies rather than listing multiple guessed squares — guessing 3-4 king/piece destinations when only 1-2 are legal burns calls on confirmed-illegal moves. If the tool already printed a legal-reply list for that exact position, your next guess must come from that list verbatim; do not invent a fresh square. Repeatedly guessing untested replies after an error already gave you the real list wastes most of a turn's budget for zero new information. — wrong notation/missing check symbol, wrong ply count, a move already played (start `<line>` from the current position), too many arguments, or a state-based precondition (e.g. "not available while in check" → switch tools first). Then immediately re-read the error message itself: it typically embeds the tool's ground-truth list of actual legal replies ("X is not a legal move here; opponent's legal replies: [...]"). Re-issue the SAME `<simulate>`/`<line>` call using exactly those listed moves instead of guessing again — this recovers the call's value with no extra budget spent. HARD RULE: after any such error, your very next call for that line must use ONLY moves drawn from the error's own legal-reply list — do not invent another guessed square even for a different ply; repeated blind guessing after the correct list was already shown has wasted 5+ calls in observed failures and left moves committed unverified.
 NEVER batch multiple unverified guessed replies (e.g., several king squares) into one `<simulate>` call hoping one lands — if an error already named the actual legal reply, use ONLY that exact move in your next call; guessing several alternatives at once when the correct one is already known wastes the entire call. That list is authoritative for the rest of this turn; never reissue a call for the same candidate with a reply not on it. Runtime check: before every new `<simulate>`/`<board>`/`<line>` call, re-scan the last 1-2 tool outputs for an explicit legal-move/legal-reply list and cross off any square/move you're about to query that isn't on it — repeating a guess an error already excluded (even a different guess on a later ply) is the same mistake and must not recur. Do NOT abandon verification and fall back to a single-ply `<board>` snapshot plus verbal justification, and never commit a move solely because it still appears in the legal-move list after your verification call errored — an error means "unverified," not implicit confirmation.

If `<simulate>`/`<line>` errors repeatedly on the exact candidate you intend to play, fix the call format (move, then a specific opponent reply drawn from `<threats>`/`<hanging>`) and keep retrying — never commit that candidate on verbal assumption while verification is still failing; if calls are exhausted, fall back to a different candidate you DID verify rather than the unverified one.

For every candidate, call `<safety>`/`<board>`/`<simulate>` and confirm explicitly in the output: (1) the intended tactical effect — gives check, wins material, or concrete threat; (2) "your side en prise" reads none (or the loss is acceptable). Never call `<simulate>`/`<line>` with placeholder or missing arguments — every call must include your candidate move AND at least one concrete opponent reply.

Verify against the opponent's worst/strongest reply, not a cherry-picked favorable one: for every candidate, simulate it against EACH forcing reply (every check and capture) listed by `<threats>` or the legal-move list — not just one — and read the resulting material/en-prise status per branch. If a guessed reply errors as illegal, treat the error as a cue that your reply set was incomplete, not just a syntax fix: re-issue the call covering every remaining legal reply before concluding the candidate is safe. When the candidate is itself a checking move (or any forcing move), batch all its legal replies into a single `<simulate>` call, e.g. `<simulate>Rf1+, Ke4, Kg4, Kg2, Ke2</simulate>`, and read the "material"/"en prise" fields for every branch — a move is only as good as its worst branch, so judge safety by the worst-case reply across ALL forcing replies, not the one you expect.

Mating-candidate checkpoint: a `#`/`+` tag in the legal-moves list (e.g., "Qh5#") is a hint to verify, NEVER proof to commit on — even if a reference/expert trace you're imitating skipped this step. Name the mating pattern in one line (which squares are covered/why the king has no escape), then make one confirming `<board>`/`<simulate>`/`<line>` call on that exact move and read the output literally stating "CHECKMATE" or zero legal replies — "gives check"/"in check" alone is NOT equivalent and means the line is unfinished (request one more ply of legal replies). If your first verification attempt errors (bad syntax, wrong reply square), fix the call and retry — a flawed-but-attempted verification is still the correct habit; do not abandon the check and fall back to committing off the symbol. For a mating *sequence* spanning multiple plies, or any move that only *looks* decisive from your own read (no tag), run a single `<line>` call through the FULL sequence to the same terminal confirmation. Skip the confirming call only if tool budget is fully exhausted.


If your first `<simulate>` call on a forcing move only returns an intermediate state (e.g., "forces king to X, only legal response") with no completed mating/material follow-up, do NOT commit on that alone and do NOT re-run the identical call again — immediately issue ONE `<line>` call chaining your move, the forced reply, and your intended follow-up (e.g., `<line>Qg8+, Rxg8, Nf7#</line>`) and require the output to state "CHECKMATE" or a concrete favorable material count before playing the first move. If a follow-up move in that chain is illegal, fix the move string from the legal-replies list and retry once — never substitute "this forces a king move" reasoning for a completed, verified line.

When a candidate involves a capture/sacrifice that invites a recapture onto a square you can capture again, do NOT stop at the first `<simulate>` reply even if it looks momentarily "down material" — extend with `<line>` or chained `<simulate>` calls (e.g., `<line>Bxf3+, Qxf3, gxf3</line>`) to play out the full forced sequence and judge by the end-of-sequence material count. Runtime checkpoint: if you don't have a tool result showing the position at least two plies deep, retry rather than playing blind.

Reminder: a confirmed CHECKMATE/STALEMATE/zero-legal-replies verdict moots any secondary en-prise flag — commit immediately; this does not extend to non-mate forcing checks, where a real en-prise flag still requires weighing risk before committing (see 'Judging Tool Output' for the full rule).

Before calling `<simulate>`/`<line>` on a checking/forcing move, first call `<safety>` (or reuse the "forcing replies"/"legal replies" field already printed by a prior tool call) to get the opponent's actual legal replies — never guess a king/piece destination square. If a `<simulate>` call errors with "is not a legal move here," treat that as a signal to re-fetch the legal-reply list, not to guess a different square.

When testing a check/sacrifice that depends on "no good reply," simulate the most natural opponent response first (e.g., the obvious recapture); if the tool returns a different forced legal reply than you expected, treat that as new ground truth and immediately re-run `<simulate>`/`<line>` with that corrected reply before judging the line sound.


When a tool call returns a decisive, unambiguous verdict (e.g., "CHECKMATE — no legal replies" or an exhaustive forced-reply list with no escape), write your commit justification as a direct restatement/paraphrase of that literal verdict — do not substitute a separately hand-derived board narrative (even a plausible-sounding one) for the tool's actual finding. The stated reason and the verified reason must be the same claim.


Format every `<simulate>`/`<line>` call exactly like a prior successful call (comma-separated move list, your move plus at least one opponent reply, no stray commas/spaces) to avoid burning budget on syntax errors; if a call errors, read the full error message for embedded info (legal-move list, ambiguity resolution, mate/check annotations) and fix the exact syntax issue rather than guessing a different move.

When relying on a specific opponent reply to justify a forcing move, run `<simulate>`/`<line>` listing that reply plus at least one alternative king/piece move in the resulting position, and confirm via the output that your assumed reply is forced (others shown illegal) or clearly the opponent's best — do not assume a reply from mental picture alone.

Before your FIRST verification call (`<board>`/`<safety>`/`<simulate>`) on any candidate, scan the full legal-move list for every check and capture and name in your WHY line at least 2 distinct candidates (not just the one you intend to verify) plus the pattern each threatens (mate net, fork, pin, overload). Only after this quick multi-candidate scan should you pick one to verify first — do not call a verification tool off a single spotted idea, even if it looks like mate, without first noting what else was available.


When a candidate creates or relies on a new asset (a passed pawn, a new threat, a square-control gain) rather than immediately winning material, do not verify it only against the opponent's capturing/most-obvious reply — explicitly `<simulate>` at least one additional opponent reply that attacks or races to neutralize that specific new asset before committing.

To confirm a reply is truly forced (not just likely), pass the candidate move plus one or more alternative replies into `<simulate>`/`<line>`; a returned "is not a legal move" error on every alternative is positive proof the remaining reply is forced — use that confirmed-forced reply as the basis for the rest of your line rather than assuming it from a mental read.

Runtime check reinforced by repeated successful play: after any `<line>` call, read the returned `status` field directly (e.g., CHECKMATE, STALEMATE, material count) and commit only if it is an unambiguous terminal result — never infer a result from your own mental image of the board, and discard/retry any line whose simulated reply is illegal or unexpected rather than assuming it worked.


When chaining a forced reply into `<simulate>`/`<line>`, always include your own planned follow-up move after that reply in the SAME call (e.g., `<simulate>Ra8+, Rb8, Rxb8</simulate>`), not just the opponent's forced reply alone — stopping one ply short means you commit without seeing the actual end-state (material/CHECKMATE/en-prise).


For quiet structural moves (pawn locks/advances, prophylactic moves) that aren't captures or checks, still call `<board>` on the candidate first to preview the resulting structure and confirm "en prise: none"/"gives check: no" before committing — verification applies to structural moves, not just tactical ones.

Never commit based on a bare `<board>` call of ONLY your own move (a pre-opponent-reply snapshot) — this shows nothing about the opponent's response. If an earlier `<safety>` call on this exact move already flagged a forcing opponent reply (e.g., "attackers 2 vs defenders 1" or a named capture of your piece), you MUST run `<simulate>`/`<line>` through that specific forced reply and read the resulting material/status before `<action>`, even if budget is nearly exhausted — a flagged-but-unsimulated forcing reply is a known risk, not a resolved one.


Every `<simulate>`/`<line>` call starts fresh from the REAL current board, never from a hypothetical position set up in a prior call — you cannot "continue" an earlier imagined line by passing only the next moves. To explore a continuation after an earlier hypothetical, re-specify the FULL move sequence from the current actual position in one call (e.g., `<line>Bxh7,Kxh7,Qh5+,Kg8,Qxg7+</line>`, not a bare follow-up assuming Bxh7 already happened). If a call returns illegal-move errors or an unexpected board/material state, STOP and re-read the returned position before trusting your plan — do not commit based on a call that silently replayed from the wrong starting position.

Before listing opponent reply squares in `<simulate>`/`<safety>`, derive which replies are geometrically possible from the board already shown (king adjacency, board edge, blocking pieces, pins) and submit only the 1-2 most-likely ones — do not pad the call with 4-5 guessed squares. If the tool responds "not legal, only X is legal," immediately analyze X next; do not submit another batch of guesses in the same family (this has burned entire calls when the correct single reply was already given).

Before submitting any candidate with a check/mate suffix, infer the correct suffix from the position: if your move leaves the opponent with zero legal replies it is `#` (mate), otherwise `+` (check) — guessing the wrong suffix (e.g. `Qe6+` when the position is actually `Qe6#`) causes a hard "not a legal move here" error. If unsure which suffix applies, drop the suffix or call `<board>`/`<line>` on the plain move first instead of guessing.
## Comparing Candidates

Before committing full `<simulate>`/`<line>` budget to deep-diving one candidate, confirm you have `<safety>`/`<threats>`-screened at least 2-3 DISTINCT candidate moves (not multiple replies within one line) — if fewer than 2-3 were screened, go back and batch-check more alternatives (checks, captures, threat-removing moves) before narrowing to one branch.

After your initial scan (checks, captures, threat-named moves), shortlist at least 2-3 distinct candidates

Never bank a trade/capture as 'equal' from piece-value intuition alone — run `<simulate>` on it and trust the printed material count; an apparently even trade (e.g., queen-for-rook-looking swap) can concretely cost material once the forced recapture is simulated. For advanced/passed enemy pawns specifically, simulate the opponent's actual recapture (not just king or other moves) and commit only if that line still leaves you ahead or even in material. — including an "obvious" flagged mate — do not fixate on or commit the first sufficient-looking move. For each candidate use `<line>` (not just an isolated `<board>` snapshot) to replay the full forced sequence to its end, and compare the resulting checkmate status / material count against the pre-move count — do not settle for a merely material-neutral or "best so far" candidate if a better-confirmed option remains unchecked. When multiple checks/captures are available, sweep them one by one through `<simulate>`/`<line>` against the opponent's actual forced reply and compare printed material counts side by side (e.g., Qf8+ Bxf8 -> 13-24, Rf8+ Bxf8 -> 17-24, Qc8+ Rd8 -> 22-24); commit only to the one with the best confirmed result.


When a `<simulate>` result reveals a forcing follow-up on your current candidate (a check, a fork, an undefended piece reachable next move), immediately spend your next 1-2 calls deepening that *same* candidate with `<line>` to a concrete material count — do NOT branch to a new, unrelated candidate. Only move to a different candidate after you've confirmed or disproved the current line's payoff.


Do not default-commit whichever candidate you've already simulated if its result is mixed (e.g., still down material, or shows "your side en prise"/"you are in check" on your own pieces) — a touched-but-unresolved candidate is not automatically better than an untested one. When a position has multiple plausible checks/captures (a sharp tactical melee) or a forcing move produces multiple distinct legal opponent replies, probe EVERY one via `<simulate>`/`<line>`/`<board>` and read the explicit "gives check"/material-count/en-prise fields before committing — not just the first or most obvious branch. If one reply's output flags a piece as "en prise" or shows an undefended blocker, go one ply deeper in THAT branch (test the recapture) rather than abandoning it for an easier-looking branch; if a call errors mid-branch, correct and retry one ply deeper in that same branch rather than pivoting to a weaker, unverified branch. Commit only to the candidate whose tool output confirms a forced, material-winning continuation.

Whenever a NEW legal-move list appears mid-sequence (e.g., after the opponent's forced reply to a check), re-scan it explicitly for every move containing "x" (all captures), rank them by the value of the captured piece (queen > rook > minor piece > pawn), and verify the top-ranked capture with `<safety>`/`<line>` before settling for a lower-value capture from your original plan. A rook or piece left undefended after a king is forced to move is a common miss — check for it specifically.


When a forced-capture/check sequence terminates with material merely equal (not a clear win), do not stop and commit there — pull up the legal-move list at that final position (or one more `<line>`/`<simulate>` ply) and scan specifically for a check, fork, or deflection-exploiting capture against the piece that was just forced to move or recapture. Only settle for the equal-material line if that extra ply check finds nothing better.


Proven opening pattern for multiple forcing candidates: batch them all into one `<safety>` call (e.g., `<safety>Ra8+, Rf7+, g6</safety>`) to compare legal-reply counts and en-prise/exchange risk side by side. Prune any candidate flagged "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK" or with more attackers than defenders immediately; among the survivors, prefer the one with the FEWEST opponent replies (simplest to verify fully) for deeper `<simulate>`/`<line>` analysis — do this before committing calls to any single candidate's full line.
Budget-priority rule: before spending calls checking exchange-risk on queen/piece sacrifices one by one, first scan the FULL legal-move list for the single check that most restricts the opponent king (fewest legal reply squares, ideally reducible to 1) — this is almost always the real winning candidate, not the flashiest sacrifice. Test THAT candidate first with one `<line>` call going two of your moves deep (your move, forced reply, your mating try) BEFORE screening weaker-looking sacrifices with `<safety>` — this preserves enough budget to confirm the final move is actual CHECKMATE (not just "in check") rather than running out mid-verification.
 Treat an opponent-reply count of exactly 1 (reported by `<safety>`) as a strong signal that candidate is the most forcing line available — prioritize following it up with `<line>`/`<board>` over candidates with more replies, even if another candidate looks superficially more aggressive. Once a forcing move's `<simulate>` output shows a clearly favorable swing on at least one branch, stay on that exact move/line and deepen it with the opponent's best-for-them reply rather than branching to unrelated quiet candidates; explicitly identify which reply is forced or best-for-opponent (not the one most convenient for you) and verify THAT branch before committing.

Track any of your own pieces flagged en prise by `<threats>`/`<hanging>` at the start of the turn across every subsequent `<simulate>`/`<safety>` call — before committing, re-read the most recent tool output's "your side en prise"/material field for the exact move you're about to play and confirm that piece's status (captured, defended, or outweighed) is factored into the final material comparison, not just your own attacking idea. Runtime check: whenever you batch multiple checks/captures through `<safety>`/`<simulate>` together, explicitly line up each candidate's risk flag (en-prise/exchange-risk) alongside its simulated material delta before picking one — do not choose based on either signal alone.
## Judging Tool Output



If `<threats>`/`<simulate>` output lists several distinct forcing opponent replies, the opponent will play their strongest reply, not a favorable one — check the candidate against the worst-case reply before committing.
 If ANY returned reply leaves your own king in check or a piece newly en prise that your plan hasn't addressed, do not commit on the other branches' good results alone — run one more `<simulate>`/`<board>` call on that specific unresolved reply and read its material/check status before playing the first move. When a forcing move's payoff depends on which square the opponent is pushed to (e.g., a king forced to move), do NOT commit after confirming just ONE reply branch — feed ALL (or, if the tool caps replies per call, all in batched calls) of the opponent's legal replies from that position into `<simulate>`/`<line>` and confirm the SAME favorable result (mate or verified material gain) holds across every branch before playing the move.

CHECKMATE overrides any secondary warning: if a `<safety>`/`<simulate>`/`<line>` output explicitly states CHECKMATE (or "opponent has no legal replies") for your exact candidate, commit it immediately even if the same output also flags "your pieces en prise after the move" or a similar exchange-risk warning — the game ends before any recapture can occur, so that warning is moot. Check first for the terminal verdict/empty legal-replies field; only when that is ABSENT does a real en-prise flag mean weigh the risk and verify the follow-up before committing.

A single `<board>` call on a check only shows material and "gives check" — it does NOT show the opponent's forced reply or whether a follow-up mates. Before committing any move intended to force mate or decide the game, run `<line>`/chained `<simulate>` through the full sequence (your move, the opponent's only/forced reply, your follow-up) and commit only once the returned status explicitly reads "CHECKMATE" (or an unambiguous winning material count) — never commit straight off a single `<board>` snapshot of the first move alone.


Do not discard a capture/check just because `<safety>` flags it as a "losing exchange" or similar risk — this flag only counts attackers/defenders, it cannot see mating patterns or follow-up tactics. If the candidate matches an identified mate geometry, immediately run `<line>` through the forced reply (skip re-running `<safety>` on alternatives first) and read the terminal status/material before deciding. When choosing BETWEEN two or more forcing candidates and one carries a risk flag, don't accept or reject it from the flag alone — run `<line>`/`<simulate>` through the forced reply for each candidate and compare the printed material counts side by side before picking. Only abandon a flagged move if its `<line>` result shows no follow-up tactic outweighing the material loss.

A "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK"/"en prise" flag only means a piece CAN be recaptured — it says nothing about whether the trade is good for the opponent. Always run `<safety>` on a candidate check/capture first, then if it's flagged, go one step further with `<simulate>`/`<board>` on the forced recapture (and the "opponent declines" branch too) and read the printed material count: a rook captured to win a queen is a material GAIN, not a risk, and must be preferred over an unverified alternative even though flagged. If the step-by-step call errors on syntax, correct and retry — never fall back to the surface flag as a substitute for this deeper check. Compare net material after the full forced sequence across all flagged candidates and commit the one with the best confirmed end-state, never the one that merely "looks" safer from the label alone.

Hard veto rule: if any `<safety>` or `<simulate>` call on a specific move has already returned a negative flag (e.g., "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK") or shown you materially worse off than before the move, treat that move as permanently rejected for this turn — never commit it later out of budget pressure or verbal rationalization ("king is undefended") that isn't backed by a fresh tool result. Search the legal-move list for a different candidate instead.


Stale-flag check: if a move was flagged risky/losing by an earlier `<safety>`/`<simulate>` call, do not commit it later without re-running that same tool on the exact current position and confirming whether the flagged capturing/forcing reply is still legal there. Never resolve a conflict between an old risk flag and a new board state by verbal assumption (e.g., "defended by X pawn") — re-verify with a tool call before committing.

Before spending calls justifying a risky candidate, check whether a same-type forcing alternative (another check/capture achieving a similar effect) reports clean safety ("your pieces en prise: none", attackers ≤ defenders at destination). If so, prefer that clean alternative over the flagged one — only invest `<simulate>`/`<line>` calls defending the risky candidate when no such safer equivalent exists.


If a `<simulate>`/`<board>` result does not explicitly contain a mate/no-legal-moves marker (e.g. it only prints material count and "you are in check: False/True"), do NOT write or imply "mate" in your reasoning or commit as if the line were proven winning — a heavy material swing alone is not checkmate. Either run one more `<board>`/`<line>` call on the resulting position to confirm the king has zero legal replies, or treat the position as unresolved and re-scan for a safer/different candidate.


Hard data overrides narrative: if the most recent `<simulate>`/`<safety>` call on the exact move you are about to play already shows the moved piece captured with a material swing worse than your current position (check the "material"/"en prise" fields), do NOT commit it on verbal rationalization — discard it and select a verified alternative with neutral-or-better material.

Final pre-commit veto check: immediately before `<action>`, re-read the MOST RECENT `<safety>`/`<simulate>` output for the exact move you're about to play. If that output carries "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK" or shows a forced capture leaving you materially down with no compensating tactic, do NOT play it — this holds even if you already started drafting the commit. Instead, return to the full candidate batch gathered earlier (from `<hanging>`/`<safety>`) and pick the next candidate with no risk tag and neutral-or-better confirmed material, verifying it with its own `<simulate>`/`<line>` before committing. A single `<board>` snapshot is NOT sufficient re-verification for a move already shown risky — it must be a fresh `<simulate>`/`<line>` on a different candidate, not a lighter-weight re-check of the same flagged one.


Hard comparison veto: if ANY `<simulate>`/`<safety>` call this turn already showed your intended move resulting in a worse material count or your own piece listed "en prise" post-move, do NOT play it — not even as a final fallback — unless you have zero other tested candidate; compare that bad result against every other candidate you tested and commit the one with the best confirmed material/safety outcome, even if it means abandoning a move you spent time analyzing.

Exception to the above: when `<safety>` flags one of YOUR OWN pieces as en prise as a side-effect of a forcing move (check/fork) — common with forks where one piece is exposed to win a bigger one — do not discard the move on that flag alone. Use `<threats>`/`<hanging>` to identify what the forcing move actually wins, then run `<line>`/`<simulate>` through the forced reply AND your follow-up capture (e.g., `<line>Nc4+, Kb5, Nxd2</line>`) to confirm the net material after both trades. Reject the move only if that simulated NET outcome (not the surface en-prise flag) is worse than the best verified alternative.

A "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK"/"en prise" flag only means a piece CAN be recaptured — it says nothing about whether the trade is good. Before discarding any flagged capture, run `<simulate>` on the forced recapture and read the printed material count: a rook captured to win a queen (or any higher-value piece for lower) is a material GAIN, not a risk, and must be preferred over an unverified alternative even though both were flagged. Compare net material after the full forced sequence across all flagged candidates, and commit the one with the best confirmed end-state — never the one that merely "looks" safer from the flag label. Concrete example of the mistake to avoid: when `<safety>` flags multiple checking candidates (e.g., Qxh3+, Qxg2+, Qh2+) as "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK", do not drop the flagged ones unexamined — simulate each (e.g., `<simulate>Qxg2+, Kxg2</simulate>`) and read the resulting material/mate status even if it confirms a material loss. If the flagged move GIVES CHECK or otherwise has only one forced legal reply, do NOT stop at the material-losing recapture — immediately extend one more ply with `<line>` (or request legal moves in the resulting position) to check for a mate or decisive follow-up before ruling the move out; a "losing exchange" warning only measures the sacrifice itself, not what it sets up. Only after seeing each flagged line's concrete end-state should you discard it in favor of the one line verified by `<line>` to end in CHECKMATE.


Proven habit: after every `<simulate>`/`<board>` call, explicitly read both the "material" line and "your side en prise" field, and track the material delta across successive calls in the same line (e.g., down 4 → down 1) — commit only once you've confirmed your side's material is not regressing, or any regression is outweighed by a verified larger gain shown in that same tool output.


Never commit a move whose own `<safety>`/`<simulate>`/`<line>` output already showed a net material LOSS with no confirmed mate (e.g., "down 3" with the king merely escaping) — do not override this with verbal reasoning like "forces king to move, then X wins" unless you run one more `<line>` call carrying the FULL claimed follow-up to an explicit CHECKMATE or a re-verified favorable material count. If that confirming call is missing, ambiguous, or shows the follow-up move illegal, discard the sacrifice and commit the best already-verified risk-free alternative instead.
## Converting the Line (Second Move)



Before playing the finishing move of a line, re-scan the current legal-move list for any direct capture of a piece the board/material-count already shows as undefended, and prefer that verified capture over an unverified speculative check or sacrifice. ALWAYS confirm the finishing move with `<board>`/`<safety>`/`<simulate>`/`<line>` before committing, regardless of remaining budget — a single `<board>` call on the finishing move showing "en prise: none" and "CHECKMATE" is sufficient — even an "obvious" final/winning move can walk into a free recapture (e.g., Qxf1+ Kxf1). The only exception: skip the extra call when a prior `<line>`/`<simulate>` call in this same analysis already verified this EXACT move (same target piece/square, same motif) to an explicit terminal status (CHECKMATE/STALEMATE/confirmed material count) and the current legal-move list still shows it legal with the target unchanged. Otherwise, never commit a finishing move on verbal justification alone.


Never skip verification on the grounds that your narrative reasoning ("WHY: ...") already explains why the move works — the final committed move (including the second move of a two-move line) must have its OWN tool confirmation (or qualify for the explicit already-verified exception above) that the opponent has no safe reply and the stated follow-up still holds; playing straight from a WHY-note to `<action>` with zero applicable tool calls is a committing error even if earlier moves in the sequence were verified. Runtime checkpoint before `<action>`: cite the specific tool-returned material count or checkmate status tied to the exact move string you are about to play; if you cannot point to one, run one more `<simulate>`/`<line>` call first — and once a `<line>`/`<simulate>` call already returned a concrete favorable result for a sequence, play that sequence's first move next rather than re-deriving or second-guessing it verbally.


After identifying a capturing or forcing candidate, always check whether the move exposes any of your own pieces to immediate recapture or fork: call `<hanging>` (or `<simulate>` with the opponent's most forcing reply) and read the "your side en prise" field post-move. Plan your follow-up (e.g., a recapture) against that confirmed exposure before committing.


After identifying a capturing or forcing candidate, always check whether the move exposes any of your own pieces to immediate recapture or fork, and before labeling any move "finishing" or "mate" in your WHY, confirm that move is the exact one you simulated for THAT side on THAT ply — never attribute a mating/finishing move to the wrong side or ply. Simulate the full chain together (`<simulate>MoveA, OpponentReply, MoveB</simulate>` or `<line>`) so the claimed finishing move is tool-tested, not inferred; if budget prevents testing it, say so explicitly instead of labeling an untested move as mate.

If `<simulate>`/`<board>` on your finishing move shows you down material or flags exchange risk, do NOT commit on a mental note like "X wins it back next move." Immediately run one more `<simulate>`/`<line>` call extending the sequence to the specific move you believe recovers material, and only keep this candidate if that follow-up's printed material count confirms recovery or gain — otherwise switch to a verified alternative (e.g., a direct recapture) before calling `<action>`.

Before calling `<action>`, re-read the exact first move of the last `<line>`/`<simulate>` call whose output you are citing as justification, and confirm the move you submit is character-for-character identical to that first move — if it differs, either re-run verification on the move you actually intend to play or play the move you already verified. Do not swap in an unverified alternative at the last step.


Before writing the final WHY/PLAY, re-read the most recent tool output for that exact candidate and verify it CONFIRMS your stated justification (resulting material matches the claim, the piece you expect to promote/capture is actually gone/still there). If the output shows a worse material count or contradicts the plan (e.g., the opponent simply captures the pawn before it promotes), do NOT commit — search for a different candidate instead of restating the disproven plan.
## Budget Discipline



Once a `<line>`/`<simulate>` sequence confirms a clearly favorable final material swing, stop scanning unrelated candidates and commit — don't burn remaining budget re-checking alternatives once a line is confirmed winning.

Never repeat an identical tool call within the same move's analysis — track what you've already queried and reuse that result. If budget is low, spend remaining calls verifying the opponent's strongest defense to your chosen move rather than exploring new alternatives; don't commit on an unverified claim (e.g., "threatens mate") cut off by budget exhaustion.


Before your first `<safety>` call, scan the full legal-move list once and gather every check and capture candidate into that single call — do not split the same scan into multiple `<safety>` calls because you forgot a candidate; only issue a second batch if the first round's results reveal a genuinely new candidate. When `<simulate>` shows that several opponent replies produce the same structural result (same en-prise status, same material, no distinguishing feature), treat them as one case: verify the finishing move with a single `<line>` call on one representative branch and commit — only spend a second `<line>` call if the simulate output actually differed between branches in a way that could change your follow-up.

Bank a confirmed fallback: once `<simulate>`/`<line>` fully confirms a concrete material/mate result for a candidate, treat it as your verified fallback and default commit. Once that fallback is banked, spend remaining budget DEEPENING or stress-testing that SAME candidate (alternate recaptures, `<hanging>` on the resulting position) rather than opening new, unrelated candidates from scratch — each new candidate costs several calls just to establish legality and rarely finishes verified within the budget that remains. Only switch away from the banked fallback if a new candidate reaches an equally complete, concrete verification showing a strictly better outcome; a partially-explored new candidate is never grounds to abandon a fully-verified one. If verifying a late candidate errors, hits illegal replies, or runs out of budget, commit the earlier verified fallback instead. Runtime check: before calling `<action>`, confirm the move you're about to play is the exact move whose `<line>`/`<simulate>` output you can cite with a specific resulting material/mate line in the actual branch that occurred — not a hypothetical branch you checked earlier. Hard veto: if the LAST `<simulate>`/`<line>` output you produced for the move you're about to play shows one of your own pieces captured with no compensating gain, do NOT commit it — fall back to the earlier fully-verified line with an acceptable or better result instead.


Do not commit off a single shallow snapshot (`<board>` on one line only): first use a breadth tool (`<safety>` across 2-3+ candidates, or `<threats>`/`<hanging>`) to compare exchange risk, then deepen the chosen candidate with `<simulate>`/`<line>` through multiple plies to a concrete material/promotion/mate outcome. If many tool calls remain unused and your only evidence is one shallow call, spend more calls before committing.


Enumerate EVERY legal opponent reply shown in your chosen move's `<safety>`/`<simulate>` output, not just the one you hope for — if any reply is still unresolved (no traced material/mate result) when budget runs low, do NOT commit that candidate; commit instead to whichever candidate you have already fully verified across ALL its reported replies. A single favorable branch does not override an unfavorable, unresolved branch reported by the same tool call. Converge early: once a candidate shows a clear tactical point and a forcing continuation, STOP generating new unrelated candidates and spend remaining budget fully verifying that ONE candidate to a concrete end state — if you've used more than half your budget without settling on one candidate, converge on the strongest one found so far. When only 1-2 calls remain, do not open a new candidate line — spend them finishing verification of the candidate already in progress; if budget runs out before that confirmation returns, do NOT default to the unverified mid-investigation move, commit instead the last candidate for which you already hold a complete, non-losing simulate/line result.


Once a forcing move is confirmed to have exactly one legal reply, extend that SAME branch one ply at a time using the legal-move list returned for the resulting position — do not fan out into 2-3 unrelated follow-up candidates (e.g., trying several different second moves) hoping one lands. Runtime check: if you've spent more than ~2 calls on one move without reaching a terminal/decisive result, continue deepening the single most-promising branch (the one already flagged in-check or materially ahead) rather than opening a new one; only abandon it for an error that proves it fails.

Tool-call budget resets every move
HARD RULE: do not play a second/follow-up move (e.g., the reply after the opponent's forced move) on a WHY-only rationale with zero tool calls this new position — even if the previous move's budget ran out and even if the move "looks obviously forced or decisive." Spend at least one `<board>`/`<simulate>` call on the new position first and read the resulting material/en-prise fields before `<action>`. — never skip verification on a move because the PREVIOUS move's budget ran out. Before any `<action>`, confirm you have made at least one tool call THIS move's budget on the exact move you are about to play; if zero calls were made this move, make one now (even a single `<simulate>` enumerating the opponent's replies) before committing.


When a candidate is a forcing move, prefer one `<line>` call carrying the full intended sequence (your move, opponent's most dangerous reply, your follow-up) over multiple separate `<simulate>` calls on overlapping king/piece reply squares — check the printed material count explicitly improves in your favor, not just "no check"/"no en prise." Before any tool call, check whether the same move + same opponent reply was already queried this move; if the answer is already in a prior output, do not re-issue it — query only unanswered reply squares, and trim your reply list to any documented per-call argument limit proactively rather than discovering it via an error.

When the legal-move list contains any checking moves, do NOT open with `<hanging>`/`<threats>` — batch all checking candidates into one `<safety>` call first, since this is the fastest route to a forced single-reply line; fall back to `<hanging>`/`<threats>` as the opener only when no checks are available.

Do not abandon a candidate that already showed a decisive, forced material swing (e.g., winning a queen for free) in order to cycle through unrelated fresh candidates — this has repeatedly burned budget on unverified moves and forced a worse default commit. Once one candidate's `<simulate>`/`<line>` output shows every checked opponent reply losing material for the opponent, exhaust that candidate's remaining branches/follow-up first; only start a genuinely new candidate if that one is disproven.


HARD RULE: every opponent-reply move passed into `<simulate>`/`<line>` must come from an already-returned legal-move/legal-reply list (never a fresh guess) — cross-check before calling. If budget is nearly exhausted, spend remaining calls completing verification of your current best-investigated candidate; never commit a move at `<action>` that has zero completed `<simulate>`/`<line>` result of its own, even under time pressure. Never commit a sacrifice/forcing move "because budget ran out" if its last verified result showed a risk flag (e.g., "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK") and material still down with no confirmed mate — fall back to the best fully-verified non-flagged alternative instead, even if it's a quieter move. Exhausted budget is a reason to stop searching, never a reason to play an unresolved risky line.


Do not bounce between 2-3 different unrelated candidates (e.g., several queen sacs) spending a few calls on each — once you start verifying a candidate, finish it (via `<line>`/`<simulate>` to a forced end-state or mate) or explicitly disprove it before switching. If you disprove your current candidate, return first to any already-identified free/safe capture (from `<hanging>`/`<safety>`) rather than opening a new risky one; running out of budget mid-verification on a third candidate while an earlier confirmed-safe capture sat unplayed is a repeated, avoidable failure.

Comparative veto: before committing, compare the candidate's verified forced-sequence material/outcome (from `<simulate>`/`<line>`) against every OTHER candidate you already tested this turn. Never commit a move whose own tool output shows a worse material result or no check/threat than an alternative you already evaluated — not even when budget is exhausted. If budget runs out before finding something at least as good as your best already-verified option, commit that already-verified option instead of the in-progress unverified one.
## Common Pitfalls



- **Unresolved self-threat lock-in**: After `<threats>` flags your own piece/square as attacked (e.g., a fork or hanging rook), explicitly name that piece/square and require every subsequent candidate to either remove it from danger, add a defender, or create an equal/greater counter-threat. Before testing a new "attacking" idea, check the most recent `<simulate>`/`<safety>` output's en-prise field — if the originally flagged piece is STILL listed there, treat the threat as unsolved and generate your next candidate specifically to fix it, not a fresh unrelated tactic.
- **Duplicate speculative probing**: Before spending a call on a new candidate, check whether the immediately preceding `<simulate>`/`<safety>` result already refuted a structurally similar idea (same target square/piece, same motif). If so, do not retest the near-duplicate — either pick a materially different candidate or return to defusing the already-identified threat. Reserve at least one call near the end of budget to simulate the exact move you intend to commit; only commit once that result shows no new en-prise pieces and non-losing material.

- **Narrow candidate cycling**: If your first 2-3 candidates (checks/captures) all come back flagged "LOSING EXCHANGE RISK" or en-prise, stop retrying variations on the same few risky moves. Widen the search across the FULL legal-move list, including quiet rook/king moves and attacks on undefended enemy pawns/pieces, before settling for a losing line.

- **Consolidating-move en-prise blindness**: When ahead on material with no forced tactic, do NOT commit the first consolidating/retreating move you think of, even a checking one. Generate 2-3 candidate consolidating moves, batch them into one `<safety>` call, discard any flagged `[LOSING EXCHANGE RISK]` or with nonzero en-prise on your moved piece, and commit only the one confirmed `attackers 0 vs defenders 0`. If `<board>`/`<safety>` on your intended move explicitly lists your own piece under "en prise," that is a hard stop — do not play it on "forces king away"-style reasoning; test the next candidate instead.
