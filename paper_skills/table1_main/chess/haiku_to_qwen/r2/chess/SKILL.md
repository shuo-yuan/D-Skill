## When NOT to Analyze: Fast Paths and Stopping Rules

Rules in this section license SKIPPING work: when a single cheap check
settles the position, when further verification cannot change the
decision, and when a line of analysis should be abandoned. Every entry
must name (a) the exact trigger that makes the shortcut safe and
(b) the condition under which the shortcut must NOT be taken. A rule
without both parts does not belong here.

## Verifying the Move: Tool Ritual



Treat the printed board/FEN, legal-move list, and <safety>/<simulate> output as authoritative — never re-derive the position, capture arithmetic, or a target square's safety by hand (recounting diagonals/files, chains of "if [side] takes... then material is...") to double-check a move; get consequences from the tool instead. Never manually add up piece values from the board/FEN: every position observation prints exact "Material: white X - black Y", and every <simulate>/<safety> line prints material deltas per branch — read those numbers directly instead of recounting. Self-check: if you write "wait, let me re-check whether X defends Y" more than once for the same square, or notice you're re-parsing the FEN or summing piece values in your own text, stop reasoning in text — your next output must be a tool call or a committed <action> tag, never more prose. This applies even when a move feels obviously forcing/standard or a hand-calculated line required a mid-calculation self-correction ("Wait, ..."). A single REVERSAL of verdict on the same defender/attacker fact (e.g. first concluding "Rg1 defends g2? No" then later "Yes, Rg1 defends g2") is itself sufficient to trigger an immediate tool call — do not wait for a second contradiction, and do not resolve the reversal by continued mental tracing.

Before committing to any capture, count attackers vs. defenders on the destination square via <safety> rather than assuming a piece is undefended because it looks unguarded. If the capture is a trade, also check whether the resulting position leaves one of your own pieces hanging to a recapture or follow-up, confirmed by simulating the reply — do not bank an apparently free capture without both checks.

When a <simulate> result shows one of your pieces "en prise" or a material deficit right after the opponent's forced reply, do not treat that as final — extend the simulate (or use <line>) one more ply to check whether that piece is itself attacking something valuable it can capture next; if it attacks the square the opponent's piece just moved to or through, simulate that follow-up capture explicitly before ruling the line out.

Runtime check: before <action>, confirm your most recent tool call in the current position was issued on the exact candidate you're about to play — a narrated intention to verify ("let me quickly check...") without an issued tool call is not verification, so call it now. This applies even to moves that look obviously forcing or mate — do not substitute a hand-traced board reconstruction (manually tracing piece lines, checking defenders in prose) for the tool call; call <safety> on the exact candidate and cite its literal output. Once a batched result shows a candidate with a clean risk profile, commit to it that turn rather than re-deriving alternatives by hand.


Hard trigger: never submit <action> in a turn where zero tool calls were issued on the current position — this applies even when a move looks obviously winning (mate, decisive material) or an engine-supplied annotation tempts you to skip your own verification.
 If you catch yourself proposing a specific verification call (e.g. "I'll use <safety>Qe5+</safety> to be sure") and then talking yourself out of it ("no need to overcomplicate," "I'll just output the move"), that reversal is itself the failure — issue the exact call you just named before writing anything else; a considered-then-skipped tool call is worse than never considering one at all. If you reach that point, or you've traced more than one full move-by-move line in prose without a tool call backing it, stop immediately and call <safety>/<simulate> on the current best candidate before writing anything else. Repeatedly reversing your own verdict on the same square/move ("wait, actually X IS mate... no, Y refutes it...") is itself the signal that prose tracing has replaced tool use — the fix is a single tool call, not another round of mental recalculation. Once you state that a candidate is the best move, immediately follow with exactly one `<action>MOVE</action>` tag and nothing else in that channel — no hedging, no second attempt; before sending, confirm your final message contains exactly one action tag directly following your stated conclusion.

Hard trigger: if you catch yourself re-reading FEN rank-strings character-by-character to recompute square colors, defenders, or material totals more than once in a turn, stop — that is the manual-re-derivation failure mode; end that turn with exactly one tool call or one <action> tag, never neither. Separately, when you need to know whether a piece attacks or defends a given square, do not derive it manually from the FEN (tracing diagonals/files in prose) — issue <safety> or <hanging> on the candidate immediately and read the "attackers vs defenders"/"en prise" fields as the answer; a mid-trace self-correction ("no that's not right", "wait...") is the signal to call the tool now, not to keep recalculating — this preserves tool budget for comparing verified candidates instead of re-deriving positions by hand.


Before the action tag, confirm the safety/simulate result you're citing was called in the *current* position on the exact move you're about to play (not carried over from an earlier turn), and that its "en prise after the move" field reads none — or, if it flags a trade, that you've priced it and accept it. This exact pairing (fresh tool call → "en prise: none" → immediate action) is the proven commit pattern; don't substitute a stale or hypothetical result for it.


When a candidate appears to win material because the target piece looks undefended, that alone is not sufficient: run <safety>/<simulate> on the exact candidate and separately read its "your pieces en prise after the move" field — a target with no defenders does not mean your own moved piece is safe (discovered checks, overlooked defenders, or pawn advances can flip this). Never commit a "wins material for free" verdict reached by mental board-reading alone.

Final pre-action checklist (last thing before writing `<action>`): confirm no enemy piece or pawn can immediately recapture on your move's destination square — cite the tool's 'en prise after the move' field, or if no tool covers it, name every enemy unit that could reach that square and rule each out. Only emit `<action>` once this check is clean.

More than one or two sentences of mental king-walk narration after a `<safety>` call is the signal a `<line>`/`<board>` call was skipped, not completed.

Proven pattern (confirmed by successful traces): for every check/capture candidate, run <safety> and read its "attackers vs defenders" / "en prise" fields before committing — if the moving piece would be lost (e.g. attackers > defenders on the destination), discard the candidate immediately without further debate; if the fields read clean (e.g. "attackers 0 vs defenders 0"), that alone confirms the destination is safe.
## Comparing Candidates

When two or more candidates both look promising, gather the same
evidence for each (one <safety> block, one batched <simulate> against
its testing replies) and choose by comparison — never commit the first
sufficient-looking move. When you generate several candidates in prose, do not discard most of them by imagined/narrated lines alone — spend tool calls testing the 2-3 most forcing/checking candidates via <safety>/<simulate> before selecting one. Runtime check: before <action>, count your tool calls this turn — zero tool calls despite having considered multiple candidates and having budget remaining is a hard failure signal, even if the move you land on happens to be correct. If one candidate is clearly the most forcing (check/capture with immediate threat), narrow to it and verify with tools rather than free-associating through several unverified alternatives. For every candidate you reject, base the rejection on a tool result, not a gut feeling ("not as clear", "seems best") — either verify it or explicitly flag the uncertainty before moving on.


When enumerating checks/captures, do not stop at the first candidate that "wins something" (e.g. a pawn) — extend every forcing candidate at least one more half-move (via <simulate>/<line>, or mentally if no tool budget) and rank candidates by depth of forcing continuation (mate > decisive material > small gain), not by which was found first.


For any candidate that captures or sacrifices material, state the exact point cost/gain (e.g. "Q(9) for N(3), net -6") before discarding it — but only discard it once <safety>/<simulate> or a stated line confirms no compensating mate or decisive follow-up. A clean material-loss verdict alone does not refute a move that also delivers forced mate.


When multiple captures are simultaneously available, do not settle for the first one that wins material or the flashiest check — list each capture's net point value and its destination-square safety (attackers vs. defenders via <safety>/<hanging>), then choose the largest verified-safe gain. A flashy check that costs material (e.g., loses the exchange) loses to a quieter capture that nets a full piece cleanly.


Hard rule: before committing any candidate, confirm at least one alternative legal move (another capture, check, or promoting pawn push) was also queried in the same or an adjacent <safety> call — a single-candidate turn is only acceptable when the legal-move list offers no other check/capture/passed-pawn push. If your first-choice candidate comes back flagged ("LOSING EXCHANGE RISK", pieces en prise), query at least one alternative and prefer a clean result over the flagged one unless the flagged move's line was separately confirmed to still win decisively. More generally, the instant your own reasoning names a concrete refutation or risk to a candidate you like (e.g. "but if White plays Rd8+ first, this loses the queen"), spend one <simulate>/<safety> call on that exact candidate against that exact reply in the same turn — do not resolve a self-identified risk by arguing both sides in prose, and never commit to or abandon a candidate whose risk you yourself raised until a tool result settles it.


When two or more candidates have each been individually hand-priced (in prose) as reaching comparable outcomes (e.g. both lines end "up 3 points" against the opponent's best reply), do not break the tie by further mental arithmetic or a vague preference ("this one directly threatens X") — run <simulate> on each against its most testing reply and let the tool's concrete resulting position/material decide.
